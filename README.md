## Overview

The Tailypo is a creature of North American folklore, particularly Appalachian and Southern oral tradition. In the standard tale, an isolated hunter or hermit shoots an unfamiliar animal in his cabin and severs its long tail, which he then cooks and eats. That night, and on subsequent nights, the creature returns to scratch at the cabin and call (variously) 'Tailypo, tailypo, all I want is my tailypo' or 'Who's got my tailypo?' The hunter's three hounds (often named in regional variants 'Uno, Ino, and Cumptico-Calico') drive the creature off on the first nights but are killed or scattered one by one, until on the final night the creature breaks into the cabin and kills the hunter. The Tailypo itself is described as roughly dog-sized, dark-furred, with yellow or red eyes, pointed bobcat-like ears, and a long thin tail; despite an animal appearance, it speaks plainly. Folklorists treat the Tailypo as a cautionary story about isolation, theft, and disrespect for wild creatures rather than as a cryptozoological claim.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Appalachian English; the name imitates the creature's repeated call 'tailypo' for its severed tail. Spellings vary widely (Tailypo, Tailipoe, Taily-po) reflecting oral transmission.
- **Meaning**: An onomatopoeic / dialect form of 'tail' used in the creature's refrain.
- **First use**: Oral tradition; the tale is widely cited in 20th-century Appalachian and African-American folktale collections.
